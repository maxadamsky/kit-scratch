## The Case Against

The load-bearing claim is that a dishonest `verified: <date>` annotation is a defect in the artifact, fixable by making the annotation smarter. The stronger reading is that the annotation was never a claim the toolkit was entitled to make, and that the whole staleness apparatus is downstream of one authoring decision that should be reversed rather than instrumented.

- **The date is a claim about the future, made at a moment when only the present was checked.** "Verified 2026-03-14" is true forever as a statement about March 14 and useless forever as a statement about today. Every candidate shape in the framing — checker, re-verification pass, shelf life — exists to repair the gap between those two readings. That gap is not a bug in the mechanism; it is the annotation format promising continuous grounding from a one-shot fetch. No expiry policy repairs it, because the reader who trusts a date will equally trust an unexpired date that quietly stopped being true. Shortening shelf life only narrows the window in which the artifact lies.
- **The framing's own success signal predicts failure.** It concedes the rule usually stays right, forbids optimizing for review quality, names "forty expired lines nobody triages" as the failure mode, and then sets the pass condition as a non-zero count of demoted-or-deleted lines after a year. That is a mechanism whose designers already believe the most likely outcome is rubber-stamping. Assumption not supported by the framing: I have no data on triage rates. But the framing supplies the base rate itself — the existing six-month re-verification offer "is declined by whoever is in a hurry, which is everyone." A mechanism that expires lines inherits exactly the same triage economics, minus the ability to be ignored silently.
- **The originating incident is a QA failure being reclassified as a lifecycle failure.** A URL was dead *the day it was written* and shipped anyway. Nothing about expiry dates, tiering, or grammar versioning catches a source that never resolved. The one concrete harm in the brief is entirely addressed by not writing an annotation for a fetch that failed — an authoring-time discipline, not a durable-state problem. Building a staleness system on top of this incident generalizes from a sample of one that the system would not have caught.
- **Cost lands entirely on people who did not ask for it.** These files live in other people's repositories, committed to their history. Expiry converts a currently-silent artifact into a recurring source of diffs, triage prompts, and agent-side "I cited an expired line" noise in every downstream project, in exchange for a benefit the framing explicitly declines to claim (not review quality; "honest artifacts as an end in itself"). That is a real value, but it is a value that can be delivered by asserting less rather than by policing more.

## What To Do Instead

**Change the authoring process: stop writing forward-looking grounding claims.** At setup, drop `verified: <date>` and the tiering vocabulary entirely, and write two things instead — the source URL verbatim where one exists, and nothing at all where one does not. A bare URL makes no claim about today; it is self-evidently a pointer the reader may follow, and it goes stale without ever having lied. A line with no URL is visibly ungrounded, which is the honest state of a model-knowledge line or a scaffold default. This delivers three of the four piles in the success signal — grounded-by-pointer, ungrounded, human-authored — with zero durable state, zero network, zero grammar versioning, and nothing to triage. It gives up the "expired" pile, which is precisely the pile the framing predicts nobody will act on. Additionally, at setup, do not emit a source line for a fetch that returned non-200; that is the actual fix for the shipped 404.

## Steelman

The strongest case against me: the framing's core insight is that dropping a claim you cannot back is *demotion*, and demotion needs a target tier to demote into — which means the artifact must be able to say "this was once grounded and no longer is," and a bare URL cannot express that. If a source genuinely moves from supporting a claim to contradicting it (the vendor-rename case in prior art), a bare pointer leaves the reader to discover the contradiction by clicking, which is exactly the thing the framing says agents never do. My proposal makes the artifact honest at write time but gives the reader no signal that anything changed since, and "the agent never clicks" is the one asymmetry that a passive pointer cannot fix.

## Strongest Statement

The defect is not that verified dates go stale; it is that a one-time fetch was ever allowed to print a date at all — a format that promises continuous grounding from a single check will lie no matter what policy governs its expiry. Every candidate shape on the table adds machinery to manage a claim the toolkit should simply stop making, and the framing's own success bar concedes the most likely outcome is annual rubber-stamping of lines that were mostly right anyway. Assert less: a bare source URL and a visibly bare line deliver the honesty goal with no state, no network, and nothing for anyone to triage.
